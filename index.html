THE STORY BEHIND THE NAME

A Journey Measured in Years, Not Miles

This is not a story of a straight road. It is a story of beginnings, detours, people who opened doors, seasons of waiting, and the decision to keep moving when the destination was still unclear.

I

The Boy from Wassa Mampong

FIRST STAR ACADEMY · WASSA MAMPONG

Before there was Moscow, before the university halls and the Red Diploma, there was Wassa Mampong: home, beginning, and the place that would remain part of me no matter how far I travelled. The people who knew me as a boy called me Dave. I am the last born and only boy on my mother's side, and the fourth son on my father's side. I grew up in a large family, surrounded by siblings and held within the world my parents had built.

My parents, Op. Kojo Amoateng and Adwoa Ntiakowaa, were farmers. Their lives were tied to the land, to the seasons, and to work that did not always promise immediate reward. Farming meant rising to meet the day, tending what had been planted, enduring uncertainty, and trusting that patience and effort might eventually produce a harvest. Those lessons were not delivered to me as formal lectures. They were lived in front of me. The farm was part of the household's reality, and I learned early that progress often begins long before anyone can see the result.

School did not remove me from that world. It gave me another one to enter. At First Star Academy in Wassa Mampong, my teachers began to recognise my promise. They saw something in my performance and encouraged my parents to keep investing in my education. Their message was not casual praise. It was a call to persevere, even when the cost was high and the future uncertain.

“Keep pushing him, even if it takes your last penny and your last breath.” — The message my teachers gave my parents

For a family whose livelihood depended on farming, supporting a child's education required more than good intentions. It required sacrifice: resources directed towards a child's needs, time given to a future that could not be guaranteed, and the faith to keep going without knowing exactly where the road would lead. The belief of my teachers strengthened the belief my parents already carried. Long before I could explain what I wanted to become, adults around me were asking my family not to let my potential go unrealised.

My connection with First Star Academy was special in another way. My father served as the school's Parent-Teacher Association (PTA) Chairman from its establishment and inauguration in 2003 until I completed my education there in 2013. He was part of the school community from its very beginning, and that made our family's connection to the institution particularly meaningful.

The school had a special arrangement for parents with five or more children enrolled at First Star Academy. Under this arrangement, the parent could select any one of the children to benefit from free education until completing their studies. My father selected me. Consequently, although First Star Academy was a private school, I received my entire basic education there without paying school fees. It was an opportunity that made a significant difference in my educational journey, and one for which I remain deeply grateful.

I would especially like to acknowledge the school's proprietor, Hon. Sly Kumi. I am grateful to him for creating the opportunity and for everything he did for us. The chance to receive an education at First Star Academy without the burden of school fees became an important part of my journey, and it is something I will always appreciate.

I also remember the classmates with whom I shared those formative years. Among them were Seth (my nephew), Augustina, Silas, Bernard, Emma, Abraham, Alphonse, Opoku, Moses, Shadrach, Agyabeng, Bismarck, Bernice, Paulina, Linda, Awunyo, Pephany, Theresah, Belinda, Acheampong, Asare, and many others. We learned together, competed with one another, grew together, and shared experiences that have remained part of my memories of First Star Academy. I may not mention every name here, but I am grateful to everyone who was part of that chapter of my life.

Academically, those years were also a period of competition and determination. I was consistently among the best students, but securing the first position was not always easy. Two classmates, Douglas and my friend Gideon (Blastee), made the competition particularly interesting. I usually finished third, sometimes second, and only occasionally came first. They pushed me to work harder and reminded me that being among the best did not mean I could become complacent.

That competition eventually culminated in my Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE). To my delight, I emerged as the best graduating student, achieving the best aggregate scores. It was a proud moment, not only because of the result itself, but because it showed me what consistent effort could produce. The boy who had often found himself in second or third place had finished his basic education at the top.

Looking back, I see that experience as one of my earliest lessons in perseverance. Excellence does not always arrive immediately, and being challenged by others does not diminish one's ability. Sometimes, the people who make the journey more competitive also help us discover what we are capable of achieving.

As a child, I dreamt of the stars. Not just the stars I could see in the night sky, but the distant worlds and mysteries beyond them. I wanted to become an astronomer and work with NASA. I imagined myself looking through a telescope, tracing constellations, studying distant galaxies and discovering things that most people could only wonder about. It was a dream that felt bigger than my immediate surroundings, but I held on to it.

I did not know then that life would take me in a completely different direction. I would eventually move from the stars above me to the lives, places and communities around me. Yet, in a way, I think that childhood curiosity never left. I still find myself looking beyond what is immediately visible, asking questions and trying to understand the bigger picture.

Perhaps I never became the astronomer I imagined becoming, but I have come to appreciate something else: the boy who once looked through a telescope, searching for constellations in the universe, would one day have his own constellation of experiences, struggles, people and places to trace.

My story would eventually take me into geography, political science, population and development, and public administration. Those subjects were not astronomy, but they also asked me to look beyond what was immediately visible: to examine how people live, how places change, how institutions shape opportunity, and how decisions made in one setting affect lives in another. The direction changed; the instinct to ask bigger questions did not disappear.

For all the distance my imagination could travel, my life remained rooted in home. During school holidays and university vacations, I returned to Wassa Mampong and to the farm. Education might take me away for a term or a year, but it did not sever the connection to the people and work that had formed me. The farm was not simply a place I came from. It was a reminder of the labour behind my opportunities and the family whose sacrifices made each next step possible.

Years later, when I would find myself far from Ghana, I would carry more than memories of a hometown. I would carry the example of two farmers who kept working, the voices of teachers who urged them to keep pushing, the generosity and opportunities that shaped my education, and the knowledge that a future can begin in a place the world may never think to look for it.

II

The City That Almost Swallowed Me

SIMMS SENIOR HIGH SCHOOL · KUMASI · 2013–2016

In 2013, after completing the Basic Education Certificate Examination (BECE) as the best graduating student at First Star Academy, a new chapter of my life began. Passing the examination opened a door, but it also meant leaving the familiar environment of Wassa Mampong. Senior high school would take me to Kumasi, a much larger and busier city with a different pace of life and a new set of expectations.

For a boy who had grown up so close to his parents and siblings, the move was not simply about attending another school. It was my first real experience of distance, independence, and adaptation. People who knew how attached I was to home sometimes wondered whether I would cope. My parents understood this concern and found a way to make the transition easier. Rather than sending me straight into the full boarding-school experience, they arranged for me to attend as a day student and stay with my elder siblings. It was a practical compromise: I could pursue the opportunity in Kumasi while remaining connected to the family support I had always known.

I enrolled at Simms Senior High School, where I studied General Arts from 2013 to 2016, with Elective Mathematics, Geography, Economics, and Government as my elective subjects.

I still remember my first few days in the classroom. Teachers would walk in, ask questions, and almost immediately, students would raise their hands and confidently provide answers. Their command of English, the way they constructed sentences, and how comfortably they expressed themselves fascinated me. I would sometimes sit there looking around and asking myself, Eii, am I even in the right room?

Coming from Wassa Mampong, where I had grown up in a much smaller and more familiar environment, I was not used to that level of confidence in classroom discussions. Constructing sentences in English, let alone expressing complicated ideas, was not always easy for me. Even when I had something to contribute, I would often choose to remain silent rather than risk saying something incorrectly and becoming the subject of laughter. I listened, observed, and sometimes wondered whether I could ever speak with the same confidence as some of my classmates.

That experience affected me more than I initially realised. Sometimes, the challenge was not simply knowing the answer but finding the confidence to express it. For a while, the classroom felt like a place where everyone else belonged more naturally than I did.

The first year also challenged the image I had carried of myself as a student. In junior high school, I had been used to performing among the top students. At Simms, surrounded by a wider field of capable classmates, I placed 31st out of 55 students. It was a position I had never expected to find myself in. For the first time, I had to confront the reality that being a good student in one environment did not automatically mean I would immediately excel in another.

That first ranking could have become a verdict on my ability. Instead, it became a starting point. Improvement did not arrive through one dramatic breakthrough. It came gradually, through hard work, adjustment, and persistence. I moved from 31st to 9th, then 7th, then 5th, and eventually reached 2nd place. Every improvement meant something to me because it showed that I was finding my feet in a new academic environment.

31st9th7th5th2nd
I did not arrive at the top because the new environment was easy. I learned how to meet it.

The numbers tell one part of the story. The deeper change was that I had begun to discover that I could adapt. The boy who had been carried by the familiarity of home was learning to find his footing in a larger world. Kumasi was not simply a city I had to survive; it was a place that taught me how to respond when my old measures of success no longer guaranteed a result.

One experience I will never forget happened when I eventually moved up to fifth position in my class. We were having a normal school day when the school siren suddenly went off. An announcement followed, instructing certain students to report immediately to the school library to meet the school administrators.

When the announcement reached our class, five names were mentioned. Four of us were boys—Francis, Stunna (Boamah), Raphael (Misty), and myself—while the fifth was Wendy, a female student who was in the boarding house. The four boys were friends, and we were all day students.

Our immediate reaction was that we must have done something wrong. We suspected that the Assistant Headmaster for Academics, Mr Don King (RIP), had reported us. We had a habit of taking some of the boarding students out to play football after school, and on several occasions, he had caught us and chased us away. Naturally, we thought our little football adventures had finally caught up with us.

But when we arrived at the library, the situation was completely different from what we had imagined. We found other students from different classes who had also been summoned. Some of them were already known as academically strong and well-behaved students.

The administrators explained that they had encountered a problem with the prefect selection process. Almost all the students who had applied for positions, with only a few exceptions, were not among the top five academically performing students in their respective classes. This raised concerns about the kind of representation the school wanted, particularly when external visitors came and prefects were often among the first students they encountered.

They had therefore decided to change their approach. From then on, anyone interested in contesting for a prefect position had to be among the top five students in their class. They announced the available positions and gave those of us who met the requirement the opportunity to put ourselves forward and contest through an election.

I remember feeling proud. I had never really imagined myself as someone who would be called into a meeting like that, especially after the difficult beginning I had experienced at Simms. Yet there I was, being recognised among the students considered academically strong enough to represent the school.

Interestingly, neither my friend nor I eventually contested for a position. Instead, we encouraged one of our friends, Qwabena Kesse, to put himself forward. He eventually became the Day Prefect.

Beyond the classroom and academic competition, one of the things I remember most about Simms is the bond we shared as classmates. We lived almost like one big family, helping one another through difficult times and looking out for each other like brothers and sisters. Of course, it was not always peaceful. We had our disagreements, arguments, and occasional fights. One particular incident between Kpantey and Adams still makes us laugh whenever we remember it.

Our Elective Mathematics teacher, Nana Poku, decided to deal with the situation in his own way. He made the two of them carry dustbins filled with waste for almost half the day. At the time, it was certainly not something they enjoyed, but it became one of those unforgettable school memories that we still tease them about today.

And then there were our little school adventures. I must confess that I was quite a master at the calculator-stealing game. Together with Mawuli and Boamah, we managed to get our hands on several calculators and mathematical set instruments belonging to other students. It was all part of the mischief that seemed normal among us at the time.

Ironically, my own calculator eventually got lost, and I had to borrow one to write my WASSCE. Imagine that! After all my calculator adventures, I ended up sitting for my final examination with a borrowed calculator.

Another person who made my time at Simms particularly memorable was our Economics teacher, whom we all called Prof. He taught us from Form One through Form Three and had a habit of observing things that sometimes left the entire class laughing.

He would often wonder why students who answered almost every question during lessons did not necessarily obtain the highest marks in his examinations. He would then point out that some students hardly spoke in class and behaved as though they were deaf and dumb, yet when he marked their examination scripts, they were among those scoring the highest marks.

He would then start mentioning names: “Who is David Amoateng? Who is Francis? Who is Raphael? Who is Boamah, also known as Stunna?”

The class would burst into laughter.

Over time, Prof developed a close relationship with us. Francis and I became particularly close to him, and we eventually became his best friends. We sometimes visited his house to help him with different things. His wife also became fond of us because of the good things Prof told her about us. She would welcome us warmly and prepare food for us whenever we visited.

His trust in us went far beyond the classroom. He could give Francis his ATM card and ask him to withdraw money on his behalf, even when he was not around. That was the level of confidence he had in us. He also entrusted Francis and me with marking examination scripts, including those of junior students, our own classmates, and even examination papers that we had personally written.

Looking back, I appreciate that relationship even more. Prof saw our abilities beyond how frequently we spoke in class. He trusted us, gave us responsibilities, and treated us with a level of confidence that made us feel valued. He was not just a teacher we remember for his lessons; he became someone who was genuinely part of our lives during those years.

In 2016, I completed senior high school and passed the West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE). I left Simms with more than an examination result. I had evidence that a disappointing beginning did not have to determine the ending, and that my position could change if I was willing to keep working. It was a lesson I would need again—at university, in employment, and later in a country where almost everything around me would be unfamiliar.

Some of the memories from these years survive in photographs: school uniforms, friends gathered between lessons, books open in the classroom, and the easy playfulness of young people growing up together. These photographs are not illustrations of a distant past; they are pieces of that past, preserved in the ordinary moments that often become precious with time.

At Simms, I remember Francis, Raphael (Misty), Adams, Etuo, Bimpong, Stunna, Mawuli, Ama Samaa, Nadjat, Ceci, Florence, Abayie, Rashida, Bright, Kodua (Koliko), John, Daniel, Debora, Gafar, Immy Darling, Joshua, Kpantey, Linda, Lawrence, Mercy, Lizzy, Wendy, Dorothy, Qwabena Kesse, and the Mampong Squad—Sarpong, Kwarteng, O5, and the rest of the crew.

I also remember my teachers: Nana Poku, Prof, Madam Eunice, and every teacher who taught, corrected, encouraged, and helped me along the way. The list cannot be complete, and any omission is unintentional; I offer it in gratitude, not as a ranking of who mattered.

III

The Vandal

UNIVERSITY OF GHANA, LEGON · ACCRA · 2017–2021

For a long time, Legon was not a university I imagined myself attending. Growing up in Wassa Mampong, with parents whose livelihoods depended on farming, I had developed a particular perception of the University of Ghana (Legon). Like many people around me, I associated Legon with wealth. It seemed to be a university for children from rich and privileged backgrounds, people whose families could comfortably afford the cost of studying there.

I knew my circumstances. I was a village boy from Wassa Mampong, raised by farming parents who had worked hard to provide for us. I honestly did not think we could afford Legon. Beyond the financial concern, I simply did not see myself as someone who belonged there. In my mind, I had already ruled it out.

Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), on the other hand, was where I wanted to go. I had spent my entire senior high school years in Kumasi, so I was more familiar with the university and had developed a particular interest in it. Part of that attraction was its hall culture, especially Unity Hall, popularly known as "Conti". I was fascinated by the all-male hall and its atmosphere. I used to listen to their morale and charging songs, and somehow, those sounds made me even more eager to become part of that university experience. I could already imagine myself there.

When the time came to apply for university, I was required to select two institutions. We first bought the KNUST application voucher. I needed a second choice, and since the University of Education, Winneba, had already closed its application window, we settled on the University of Cape Coast (UCC).

But even that did not go as planned.

When we got to the internet café to complete the application forms, my niece, Cobby, noticed something was wrong. The bank teller had mistakenly given us a UCC College of Distance Education voucher. I wanted to pursue a regular degree programme, not a distance education programme. We therefore had to return to the bank and exchange the voucher.

It was during that unexpected interruption that Cobby suggested something I had never seriously considered: "Why not try Legon?"

His suggestion was simple. We could apply and see what happened.

I eventually applied to the University of Ghana alongside KNUST. But the news of my admission came in a rather unexpected way.

It was Monday morning, and I had already prepared to go to the farm. I heard my father's phone ringing and went to answer it. It was Cobby. His first words were, "Congratulations! Legon has offered you admission!"

I could hardly believe what I was hearing. Cobby explained that the admission message had arrived the previous night, on Sunday, but he had only managed to inform us that morning. Since he had used his own contact details for the application process, the notification had come directly to him.

I was overwhelmed with excitement. I quickly ran to share the news with my parents, and the happiness stayed with me throughout the day. What had started as an ordinary Monday, with plans to go to the farm, had suddenly become one of the happiest days of my life.

Then, on Wednesday, Cobby called again with another piece of news: I had also been offered admission to KNUST.

By then, however, I had already made up my mind. Legon had called, and I was going. After all, who ignores Legon?

I was fortunate to receive admission into my first-choice programme combination: Geography & Resource Development, Political Science, and Dance Studies. At Legon, students initially enrolled in three subjects, dropped one at Level 200, and later decided at Level 300 whether to pursue a major and minor combination in the remaining two. I eventually chose a combined major in Geography & Resource Development and Political Science, dropping Dance Studies at Level 200.

My father initially preferred that I stay in Kumasi and attend KNUST. His concerns were understandable. Accra was far from home, and the cost of living there was considerably higher. My parents had always valued having me close to the family, and they worried about how I would adjust to another unfamiliar environment. However, the decision was ultimately mine, and they were willing to support me. We eventually settled on Legon without much difficulty.

That decision marked a turning point in how I saw myself. The university I had once written off as a place beyond my reach had offered me a place. For the first time, I began to see myself differently. I could be a Legonite too. I did not have to come from a wealthy family or fit the image I had constructed in my mind to deserve a place there.

Going to Legon also meant that I would be visiting Accra for the first time. I had never travelled such a long distance before, so I could hardly imagine what the journey would be like. When I eventually arrived, I was fascinated by the city. It was a completely different experience from anything I had known.

My first experience of the University of Ghana campus was during the orientation programme in August. I went there with Cobby, who accompanied me on that first day. As soon as I arrived, I began to understand why Legon carried the reputation it did. The architecture, the layout of the campus, the beautiful and conducive environment—everything seemed extraordinary to me.

I remember looking around and thinking, "Eii, so me too, I have finally come to Legon!"

I was overwhelmed, probably close to tears of joy. I had never imagined that I would one day find myself on that campus. It was difficult to believe that I was actually there.

On our way to the Great Hall, where the orientation was taking place, we passed Commonwealth Hall. Cobby pointed it out and explained that it was an all-male hall. He warned me that the residents were fond of fooling around and misbehaving. As we passed, I could hear them shouting and yelling at almost everyone making their way towards the Great Hall. Little did I know that I would eventually become a resident of that very hall!

I stayed in Osu during the remaining two days of the orientation programme and travelled to campus from there. Those journeys became part of my early experience of living in Accra and finding my way around the university. Later, when I finally secured accommodation in Commonwealth Hall, my experience of Legon would take another interesting turn.

Securing accommodation at the University of Ghana was another challenge. Hall accommodation was highly competitive, and by the time I realised that we were supposed to apply for a hall of residence, almost all the halls were already full. Fortunately, a lecturer from the University's Sports Directorate came to my assistance and helped me secure accommodation in Commonwealth Hall.

I went through the registration process, after which I was shown my room and assigned bed. That was when I met my roommates, Michael (RIP), who was also a first-year student, and Innusah, who was in his final year. They welcomed me warmly, making my arrival much easier. And just like that, my life in Commonwealth Hall began.

Even today, I consider Commonwealth Hall one of the most remarkable architectural designs I have ever seen. Its structure, layout and overall appearance are simply fantastic. It is one of those places you have to see for yourself to fully appreciate.

Commonwealth Hall was an all-male hall, and I quickly discovered that living there was going to be an experience of its own. I moved in on a Sunday, and by Tuesday night, I witnessed something I had never experienced before: Charging Night.

It was a night when the gallant men on the hills gathered to sing what we called Twεsple—a collection of rather profane, humorous and spirited morale songs that formed part of the hall's culture.

Honestly, I could not believe that grown men, many of them university students, could gather with so much enthusiasm to sing such songs. I was initially surprised by the whole experience. It was completely different from what I was used to.

But the atmosphere eventually got to me. Before long, I found myself enjoying the songs and joining in. Interestingly, the experience reminded me of the singing culture I had enjoyed at Conti. I had always loved listening to morale songs, so although Commonwealth's version initially surprised me, it did not take long for me to settle in. Even today, I still find myself singing some of them.

Interestingly, choosing Legon did not mean that my fascination with Conti came to an end. If anything, it followed me there. I still loved listening to their morale and charging songs. My roommate, Michael (RIP), found this particularly amusing. He would sometimes tell our colleagues and friends that I had spent a year in Conti Hall before coming to Legon. That was how much I loved their songs!

Eventually, the name Conti became attached to me in Commonwealth Hall. The Vandals began calling me "Conti", and before long, I became known as "Vandal Konti". It was a nickname that many of the Vandals who knew me used regularly.

The funny thing is that, even today, many of the colleagues and friends I made at Legon do not know me by my full name, "David Amoateng". To them, I am simply Conti.

Looking back, I have come to realise that the way people outside Legon perceive the university can be quite different from the reality and mindset of those of us who study there. I had allowed an assumption about the institution to become an assumption about myself. I had convinced myself that I was not a fit before I had even given myself the chance to try.

I have never regretted choosing Legon. What I once thought was not meant for someone like me has become one of the proudest chapters of my life.

And today, I say it without hesitation: I am a proud Legonite!

Life certainly has a funny way of working things out. I went to Legon after initially wishing to be at KNUST, but somehow, a little piece of KNUST's Conti culture found its way into my identity at Commonwealth Hall. I may have missed out on becoming a Conti in Kumasi, but I became Vandal Konti in Legon. And that, in its own way, is a story worth telling.

From 2017 to 2021, I studied for a Bachelor of Arts in Geography & Resource Development and Political Science. The combination gave me ways to think about both place and power: how land and resources are used, how societies organise themselves, and how political decisions shape the distribution of opportunities and burdens. My interests were still developing. At one point, I had hoped to pursue law, and the admissions route to the Ghana School of Law occupied my thinking between 2018 and 2020. The process reshaped my plans. I returned my attention to Geography, a discipline that would eventually connect my academic training to questions of land, livelihoods, resource extraction, and environmental governance.

University was not only about choosing a discipline or earning grades. It was also about learning how to inhabit a larger intellectual and social world. At Legon, I became a member of Commonwealth Hall, the all-male traditional hall whose students and alumni are known as Vandals. It was a family I had not imagined joining, yet it became one of the most meaningful fraternities of my life.

To outsiders, the word Vandal can suggest destruction. To those who understand the hall, it carries a different meaning: a brotherhood built around being one another's keeper. The VANDAL identity is often explained through six traits—Vivacious, Affable, Neighbourly, Devoted, Altruistic, and Loyal—and the hall motto, Truth Stands, expresses a sense of identity that reaches beyond campus. Commonwealth Hall has been home to people who have gone on to serve in public life, law, academia, sport, faith communities, banking, business, and many other fields. For me, its greatest significance was personal: the friends who became family, the solidarity, and the knowledge that I belonged to a community whose ties could travel with me.

Beyond the brotherhood of Commonwealth Hall, some of my earliest and most meaningful friendships at Legon began elsewhere. One of the first people I became friends with was Silas, the founder of the IANJAP poetry community. I met Silas during my early days on campus. Silas recognised that I had ideas and a voice that could become more confident if I was encouraged to use them. That encouragement mattered. Confidence is sometimes described as an individual quality, as if people simply possess it or lack it. My experience was different: another person helped create the conditions in which I could practise speaking, contribute to group discussions, and become more comfortable expressing what I thought.

Through Silas and the IANJAP community, I encountered people and spaces that expanded my social world. The importance of those connections was not limited to the immediate friendships they produced. They helped me understand that personal growth often happens in company—that somebody else can notice a strength before you know how to name it, and that a community can give you room to develop a capacity you might otherwise leave unused.

Then, in 2020, COVID-19 disrupted university life. The campus emptied, familiar routines broke apart, and the ordinary ways students built relationships and studied together were interrupted. Masks, uncertainty, and distance became part of everyday life. The photographs from that period carry a different mood from the graduation portraits that would follow: a reminder that this degree was completed through disruption, not in spite of a perfectly smooth university experience. Digital communication became a substitute for the physical closeness of campus. Friendships had to be maintained across distance, and academic life had to adjust to a crisis no student had planned for.

When the university reopened, the rhythm was still unusual. The double-track arrangement separated groups of students into different periods, including the 100 and 400 levels and the 200 and 300 levels. The university experience that might once have unfolded in a more continuous way became compressed and divided. I had to continue through a disrupted academic landscape, adapting once again rather than waiting for circumstances to become ideal.

I completed the degree. In November 2021, I graduated from the University of Ghana with First Class Honours, earning a final grade point average of 3.62 out of 4.00. The university's January 2022 congregation record carried the achievement in formal print: Bachelor of Arts, Geography & Resource Development and Political Science, First Class Honours. It was a major achievement, but it also closed a chapter that had changed me. Legon had been an unexpected destination, a place where I refined my academic direction, found community, developed confidence, and learned to keep going through disruption.

There are too many people to fit into a single paragraph, but their names belong somewhere in this story. At Legon, I remember Silas, Sammy, Zuta, Syram, Ben, Julius Djaba, Joyce (Nhyi), Julie (my Mum), Diana, Mike, Nana Ama, Irene, Juanita, Jennifer, Bridget, Kelvin, Keziah, Aisha, Precious, David, Koomson, Nina, Helena, Mandy—among course mates, group mates, and friends. I remember the Commonwealth Hall brothers (the V-Mates) who made the hall feel like family: Affram, Dkay, Illegal, Kizito, Martino, Kreamy, Regi, Nat, Donne, Afari, Prosper, Micky, Pioto, Richmond, Abu, Chase, Kaeff, Jarvis, John, Fabio, Peterson, Facebook, Mandamus, Buti, Ebern (ACA 2), Emma, Seniorman Listo, Ibra, Inusah, Xavier, Alex, Carlos, Michael, Lebron, Jessie, Squitto, AB, Stanley, Gideon, Slim, Gilbert, SP, Hammond, Henry, SNR Ben, Leslie, Lyrics, Muscles, Negus, Muniru, Shef, Sly, Trigger, Bernard, Kobby Vibes, and many others.

Michael, my roommate in Levels 100 and 200, is remembered here with love: may his soul rest in peace. These friendships made university more than lectures and grades; they made it conversations, shared pressure, laughter, and people I carried with me beyond Ghana.

My study group was its own circle, and I want to remember it distinctly: Sammy (group founder), Silas, Zuta, Ben, Mike, Nhyi, Julie, Diana, Nana Ama, and Maryann. We spent long hours working through assignments, preparing presentations, and helping one another make sense of university life. Some names naturally appear in more than one part of my memories because friendships overlapped, but each group had its own place in my experience.

Commonwealth Hall was a different kind of classroom. Its lesson was brotherhood: being a V-Mate meant being part of a community that looked after its own. The familiar greeting, "V-Mate!" and the response, "Sharp!", carried a sense of recognition that could make a large university feel smaller. People may hear the word "Vandal" and think of vandalism; I came to understand it as a name attached to loyalty, neighbourliness, devotion, altruism, and a shared conviction that truth stands. I had not expected to join this fraternity. I left Legon knowing it had become one of the families I would carry with me.

I had once imagined the universe through a telescope. Now I had a degree that trained me to study the relationship between people, places, resources, and political life. My interests had changed shape, but the impulse to look closely at the world—and to ask why it worked as it did—remained.

IV

The Long Wait

NATIONAL SERVICE · PRESEC LEGON · 2021–2022 · EARLY PROFESSIONAL LIFE

Graduation is often presented as the moment when effort finally turns into opportunity. For me, it was an achievement and a beginning, but it did not immediately provide a clear next step. I had completed a demanding degree with First Class Honours. Now I had to work out how that achievement would translate into employment and a future beyond university.

My first responsibility after graduation was national service. From 2021 to 2022, I served at Presbyterian Boys' Secondary School (PRESEC), Legon, supporting teaching and assessment in social science subjects. To be honest, teaching was not something I had particularly wanted to do. But national service was a call, and like every other graduate, I had to respond. Besides, being posted to PRESEC, arguably one of the best senior high schools in Ghana, was something I was proud of.

The experience turned out to be more enjoyable than I had initially expected. One thing I appreciated was the flexibility of the teaching schedule. Once I had completed my lessons and other responsibilities for the day, I could sometimes arrive later or close earlier. That arrangement gave me some room to manage my time. I also enjoyed helping to prepare students for school programmes and trips. These activities gave me the opportunity to interact with the students beyond the classroom and experience another side of school life.

One particular experience I remember was a trip I led to Aburi Girls' Senior High School. It was interesting to watch the boys interact with the girls. You could almost see the excitement in them, especially as they got the opportunity to chat and spend time with students from their sister school or partner school. I could imagine what life in an all-male secondary school must feel like, having experienced something similar through my years in Commonwealth Hall. Perhaps I understood their excitement a little better because I knew what it meant to live and socialise in an environment dominated by men.

My experience at PRESEC also gave me a different perspective on education. I had once been the student whose teachers encouraged my parents to keep pushing. Now I was on the other side of the classroom, supporting young people as they prepared for their own futures. The students accorded us the respect they gave their teachers, and that made us proud. It was a reminder that, regardless of our status as national service personnel, we had a responsibility and a place in their lives.

There was also the question of where I would stay during my service year. I remained in Commonwealth Hall throughout the period, thanks to Abu, my former roommate from Level 300. We had shared a room before COVID-19 disrupted campus life and eventually brought many of our university routines to an end. During my national service, we found ourselves living together again. Abu hosted me throughout the entire period without charging me a penny. That kind of generosity is not something I take for granted. I remain deeply grateful to him and to Ben for making that period easier for me.

Life during national service was not always comfortable financially. One of the memories that still makes me smile is our regular visits to the dining hall, where we went as teachers to get our meals. I remember going there with Rodriguez (OLR), Lawrence, and Williams. There was sometimes a little awkwardness and shyness about the whole arrangement, but we had to manage. Our national service allowance was GH¢559, and it was certainly not something to write home about. We had to make the best of what we had and find ways to get through the month.

Looking back, those dining hall visits were part of the practical realities of being a young graduate trying to make ends meet. We had degrees, responsibilities, and aspirations, but we were also learning how to survive on a modest allowance. There was no shame in making use of the support available to us. We were simply doing what we needed to do.

One of the most rewarding parts of that experience was seeing the students move on with their lives. Many of the young people I taught have since gone ahead to pursue their undergraduate studies. It is satisfying to know that the classroom was one small part of their journey before they moved on to the next stage.

There is one student in particular, Elliot, with whom I have remained in contact since my PRESEC days. Our relationship has grown beyond that of a former teacher and student. He has become my mentee and, in many ways, a brother from another mother. He is currently studying at the University of Professional Studies, Accra (UPSA). I am proud of the person he is becoming, and I value the fact that our connection has continued long after my national service ended.

At PRESEC, Madam Ophelia became more than a supervisor. She was a mentor and, in many ways, a mother to me. Her guidance helped me navigate that first professional responsibility, and her place in this story reflects the kind of care that can shape a young person's confidence long after a formal role has ended.

When national service ended, the next step did not arrive immediately. There was a long period of uncertainty—around eight months in which I applied for opportunities and waited for something to take shape. The gap between academic achievement and a stable professional path can be difficult to explain to people who see only the final certificate. A degree records what has been accomplished; it cannot guarantee how quickly the world will make room for the graduate who holds it.

For me, that period required a different kind of endurance from the one I had needed at school. There was no class ranking to improve, no examination timetable that made the next milestone clear. There were applications, uncertainty, and the difficult experience of watching time pass without knowing when an opportunity would come. The waiting did not erase my achievements, but it tested what I believed those achievements could make possible.

Eventually, I secured a role as a Risk and Compliance Officer at Pacific Savings and Loans PLC in Kumasi. The opportunity came with help from Ohemaa (Korkhor), my girlfriend, and her brother Ken, who helped open the door. Their role belongs in this part of the story because progress is rarely the product of effort alone. People make introductions, offer encouragement, and help others reach opportunities they might not be able to access by themselves.

In my role, I worked with regulatory compliance, operational risk assessment, internal audit, branch monitoring, compliance reporting, and related control processes. My department head, Mr Kofi Poku, and senior colleague, Kwame Danu, were important figures in that chapter. I also remember the then Managing Director, Mr Seth Owusu-Brobbey (may his soul rest in peace), who once told me that he had seen great things in me from the day I came for the interview. Those words became a source of encouragement in a period when I was still finding my professional footing.

I am grateful as well to Madam Eunice Acheampong, the HR Manager; Mr Francis Tano, the Finance Manager; Danny, the Accountant; Kenneth Acheampong, the then HR Officer; Josephine, Operations Officer; Mike, Accounts Officer; Aseda Boateng, IT Officer; Mr Nurudeen, Kumasi Main Branch Manager; and colleagues at the head office and across the various branches who supported my work and my stay at Pacific Savings and Loans. This acknowledgement is intentionally broad: a workplace is made by many people whose help does not always appear in a job description, but whose presence can make a difficult season manageable and meaningful.

I was grateful for the opportunity and took the work seriously. Yet employment did not extinguish my desire to continue studying and to build a life that connected more directly with my academic interests. I had earned a first-class degree, but I did not want that achievement to be the final measure of what I might become. The next chapter had not yet announced itself. For a time, I had to work, wait, and keep the possibility of a different future alive.

What I could not yet see was that a relationship from my past would soon help make that future more concrete.

V

Zeroman’s Call

GHANA → MOSCOW · NOVEMBER 2023 – SEPTEMBER 2024

In November 2023, a call came from someone who had known me long before university, employment, or international ambitions entered the picture. Zeroman, whose given name is Douglas Adjei, was my primary and junior high school classmate, an academic rival, partner in crime, and a brother in Christ. Our history included competition: Zeroman was the classmate who made it difficult for me to claim the first position in class. But the rivalry was only one part of the relationship. Beneath it was a friendship that had survived the years, even when we were no longer in regular contact.

Interestingly, we had not spoken over the phone for a long time. We occasionally saw each other's WhatsApp status updates, so we were not entirely disconnected, but neither of us had made the effort to have a proper conversation in a while. Then, unexpectedly, he called.

“Why are you still in Ghana after graduating with first class?” — Zeroman, November 2023

The question landed differently from ordinary encouragement. It asked me to look again at a situation I had begun to accept. I had a first-class degree and professional experience, but the path to further study abroad did not seem obvious. I did not feel I had the international connections or the clear route that could turn the idea into a practical plan. Over time, the absence of a visible route had begun to feel like evidence that there might be no route at all.

Furthering my education abroad had always been one of my major ambitions. In fact, it had become one of my prayer points for 2023. I was determined to pursue a master's degree outside Ghana, and I was not simply waiting for an opportunity to appear. I had already taken matters into my own hands.

Before Zeroman's call, I had independently applied to six universities for master's programmes: Penn State University, Brunel University, the University of Leeds, the University of Aberdeen, the University of Manchester, and the University of Twente. I was determined to make my dream of studying abroad a reality, and I was doing everything I could to create that opportunity. Eventually, I received admission offers from all six.

However, gaining admission was only one part of the journey. Funding was the bigger challenge. Some of the opportunities came with partial funding. Twente's funding offer covered only about 70% of the costs, while the UK options generally offered around 50% or, in some cases, no funding at all. As an international student, the remaining financial commitments were too substantial for me to manage comfortably. I knew I wanted to study abroad, but I also knew that I needed a realistic financial arrangement that would allow me to pursue the degree without placing an unbearable burden on myself or my family.

It was during this period that Zeroman introduced me to HSE University in Moscow. Before that conversation, I had never even heard of HSE. He brought the opportunity to my attention, and together we worked on the application. HSE therefore became my seventh university application. I eventually received an admission offer, alongside the six others.

What made HSE particularly special was the funding opportunity. I was awarded the Russian Government Scholarship, which covered full tuition, accommodation, and a stipend. Unlike the other offers, this package provided the financial support I needed to pursue my master's degree without the overwhelming burden of additional costs.

And just like that, Russia became a real possibility. The conversation that began with Zeroman asking why I was still in Ghana after graduating with First Class Honours had opened a door I did not even know existed.

But the decision was not as straightforward as receiving an admission offer and booking a flight.

When Zeroman first told me about the possibility of going to Russia, I was genuinely shocked. I had always wanted to study abroad, but Russia was not necessarily the destination I had imagined. At the time, the special operation in Ukraine, widely referred to as the Russia–Ukraine War, was a major concern in international news. I had questions about safety and what it would mean to relocate to a country involved in such a serious international situation.

I had mixed feelings. On one hand, here was the opportunity I had been praying and working towards: a fully funded master's degree abroad. On the other, I was worried about the implications of moving to Russia. I even wondered how I would explain the decision to my parents. I knew they would have concerns, and I understood why.

Throughout that period, Ohemaa (Korkhor), my girlfriend, was a major source of encouragement. She supported me, listened to my concerns, and helped me approach the opportunity with a more open mind. Eventually, I found the courage to discuss it with my parents.

Another person whose advice was important was Cobby. Having completed his master's studies in Israel and currently pursuing his PhD in Australia, he had considerable exposure to international education and the experience of studying abroad. His perspective and encouragement helped me think more carefully about the opportunity. His advice, alongside Ohemaa's support, helped me navigate my parents' concerns and explain why this opportunity mattered to me.

My parents eventually agreed. The concerns were understandable, but they also knew how much furthering my education meant to me. I was not making the decision carelessly. I had a clear academic objective, a scholarship that covered the major costs, and a determination to make the opportunity count. The fear was real, but it was not enough to deter me from pursuing what I had worked so hard to achieve.

In September 2024, I finally left Ghana for Moscow to begin my master's studies at HSE University. The journey was more than a flight between two countries and continents. It meant leaving familiar people, places, food, routines, and the everyday signs of home. It meant moving from a farming community in Ghana to a large Russian city whose climate, language, and social rhythms were unfamiliar.

Fortunately, I did not make the journey entirely alone. I travelled with Ofori (DJ General), George, and Rashid. We were all heading to HSE University and had each secured scholarships. Interestingly, we had not known one another for very long. We met through the application process, including university webinars and interactions at the Russian Embassy in Ghana. What began as a connection around applications and scholarship opportunities eventually brought us together on the same journey to Moscow.

As I left Ghana, my dominant feeling was excitement. I was finally going to achieve one of my biggest academic ambitions: pursuing postgraduate studies abroad. The journey represented the fulfilment of a prayer point I had carried throughout the year. But beneath that excitement was also the awareness that I was entering a completely unfamiliar environment. I was leaving behind everything I knew and stepping into a life I had never experienced before.

The cold was among the first things I had to confront. Wassa Mampong, Kumasi, and Accra had not prepared me for Moscow's winter conditions. The environment made the difference between home and the new country impossible to ignore. Beyond the weather, there was the work of learning how things functioned, navigating unfamiliar systems, and finding my place in a setting where the cues I had relied on in Ghana could no longer guide every decision.

But I did not arrive to face all of this alone. Zeroman was waiting at the airport. He helped me get to the dormitory, complete the registration process, and deal with essential arrangements. Those practical acts mattered. When a person arrives in a new country, help with accommodation and administration is not a small gesture; it can make the first days less overwhelming and turn a strange city into a place where life can begin.

He met me at the airport and helped me settle into the dormitory. In that moment, friendship became practical: someone was there to help me begin.

My move to Moscow was not the work of one person alone. My parents' sacrifices, the teachers who encouraged them, the confidence I developed at Legon, Ohemaa's support, Cobby's guidance, and Zeroman's intervention all belonged to the longer chain of events that brought me there. I also carried the encouragement and companionship of the friends who travelled with me. Each person contributed something different to the journey.

Still, Zeroman's call was a turning point. He introduced me to a university I had never known about and helped me take the next step towards an opportunity that eventually brought me to Moscow. The boy who had once competed with me for the first position in class was now part of the reason I was beginning a new academic chapter thousands of kilometres from home. It reminded me that friendships can take unexpected turns, and that sometimes the people who have known us longest can open doors we did not even know existed.

I had left Ghana with questions, fears, and expectations. I did not know exactly what awaited me in Moscow. But I knew why I had come. I had worked towards this opportunity, prayed for it, and finally taken the step. The next chapter of my life was beginning.

VI

HSE Is Not for the Weak

HSE UNIVERSITY · MOSCOW, RUSSIA · 2024–2026

Arriving in Moscow was a milestone, but arrival and belonging are not the same thing. I had reached the place I had worked towards, yet the work of becoming a student in a new country was only beginning. HSE University—the National Research University Higher School of Economics—placed me in a new academic environment and asked me to grow beyond the habits and assumptions I had carried from home.

My programme was the Master’s in Population and Development. The field of study was 38.04.04 Public Administration. The distinction matters: Population and Development was the master's programme, while Public Administration was the field of study classification. The programme brought me into a wider conversation about population processes, development, institutions, and public policy, extending the questions that had begun to take shape during my undergraduate studies.

The transition demanded adjustment on several fronts. The climate was harsh compared with the Ghana I knew. Familiar routines were gone, family was far away, and the language and culture of daily life could make even ordinary experiences feel unfamiliar. Academic work had its own demands, and I had to find ways to meet those demands while building a life in a different country. The independence that had once meant travelling from Wassa Mampong to Kumasi now meant learning to manage an international life at a much greater distance from home.

There were difficult moments. Homesickness could make the distance feel heavier; academic pressure could make the next task seem larger than the one before it. The people whose encouragement had supported me through earlier stages were no longer physically close. Ohemaa, my family, and the friends who knew my history were in Ghana, while I was trying to establish myself in Moscow. The achievement of getting there did not make the experience effortless. In some ways, it made the responsibility feel greater: I had crossed so much distance, and I wanted the sacrifice behind that journey to mean something.

At such moments, the earlier chapters of my life became more than memories. I had been the student who placed 31st before working my way to 2nd. I had navigated a university route I had not originally planned, adjusted to COVID-era disruption, completed a first-class degree, and endured a long wait for employment. None of those experiences had guaranteed an easy next step. Each had taught me that a difficult beginning did not have to settle the final outcome.

My studies also gave me the opportunity to bring my academic interests into sharper focus. My master's thesis examined The Impact of Artisanal and Small-Scale Gold Mining on Agricultural Livelihoods in Wassa-Amenfi East, Ghana. The research connected questions of mining-related land-use change with agricultural productivity, household income, food security, and the ways rural households respond to pressure on their livelihoods. Through household research, quantitative and qualitative analysis, and supporting spatial evidence, I examined a set of questions rooted in Ghana but connected to wider debates about resource extraction, environmental change, governance, and development.

The choice of topic also brought my academic journey back towards the landscape that had shaped my earliest life. I had grown up in a farming family, watching the labour and uncertainty that accompany work on the land. My research on mining and agricultural livelihoods was not simply an abstract exercise in environmental policy. It asked how changes in land and resource use affect the conditions under which people make a living. The questions were academic, but the stakes were recognisably human: land, work, income, food, and the ability of households to adapt when their surroundings change.

Alongside the thesis, my research experience included an internship examining everyday practices and discourses around artisanal and small-scale gold mining in Ghana, quantitative work on fertility and female education using Ghana Demographic and Health Survey data, and comparative public-administration research on responses to population ageing. Together, these projects show a widening set of interests—from rural livelihoods and environmental governance to demographic change and public policy—held together by a common concern with how social conditions and institutions shape people's lives.

My time at HSE also formed part of a broader development as a researcher. The work required me to move between questions, evidence, and methods: to consider what household data could show, what qualitative material could help explain, what policy documents revealed about institutions, and where spatial evidence could support a social-science argument. My interest in GIS and remote sensing remained useful as supporting tools, but the central questions were increasingly about people, governance, livelihoods, and the consequences of environmental change.

By June 2026, I had completed the Master's in Population and Development at HSE University with Distinction, earning a GPA of 8.42 out of 10 and receiving a Red Diploma / Master's with Honors. The achievement was significant in its own right. It also carried the meaning of everything that had come before it: the farm in Wassa Mampong, the teachers who urged my parents to keep investing in me, the climb through the rankings at Simms, the uncertain path to Legon, the first-class degree, the months of waiting, the professional experience in Kumasi, the call from Zeroman, and the courage I needed to begin again in Moscow.

My HSE years were made possible not only by the scholarship or the university itself, but by the people who helped me learn, adjust, and belong. Professor Arnab Roy Chowdhury supervised both my internship project and my thesis. He is now also my academic collaborator, and we are working together on research papers. I am grateful to the Population and Development programme study office and academic staff, especially my programme supervisor Alexander Larionov, programme mentor Professor Ilya Emorlin, and programme manager Ekaterina.

I also carry warm memories of HSE Dormitory 1 and the people who shared those two years with me. To my roommates and dormitory friends BOS, Habib, Henry, Akwesi, and Rejoice, thank you for sharing everyday life through my first and second years. To my classmates and the people with whom I did most of my assignments, group work, and presentations—Ofori (DJ General), George, Andrews, Vanessa, and Seedy L.—thank you for the collaboration and support. I have not forgotten Mariam, Archer, Bridget, Apostle, Wess, Vicky, Kelvin, Brilliant, Feddy, James, and all my other colleagues and course mates. Each of you is part of what made Moscow a place where I could grow.
